# A Smartphone Coaching Programme for Blood Pressure Control: A Randomized Controlled Trial

## Abstract

Uncontrolled hypertension is a leading cause of cardiovascular disease. We conducted a randomized controlled trial to test whether a smartphone coaching programme improves blood pressure control in adults with hypertension treated in primary care. A total of 412 patients were randomly assigned to the coaching programme or to usual care and followed for six months. The primary outcome was the change in systolic blood pressure. Systolic blood pressure decreased by 9.4 mmHg in the intervention group and by 4.1 mmHg in the control group, a difference of 5.3 mmHg (95% confidence interval 3.1 to 7.5). Medication adherence and home monitoring frequency also improved. The programme was well accepted, and the results support integrating digital coaching into routine hypertension care in primary care clinics.

## 1. Introduction

Hypertension affects more than one billion adults and remains poorly controlled in many health systems [1]. Lifestyle change and adherence to medication are central to treatment, but clinic visits leave little time for coaching [2]. Mobile health interventions have shown promise in small studies [3], yet evidence from adequately powered trials in primary care remains limited [4].

This study aims to evaluate whether a structured smartphone coaching programme, delivered for six months, lowers systolic blood pressure compared with usual care. Our objective was to provide trial evidence that can inform the adoption of digital coaching in primary care.

## 2. Background

Previous randomized trials of text-message reminders reported modest improvements in adherence [3]. Home blood pressure telemonitoring combined with pharmacist support reduced blood pressure in selected populations [5]. A systematic review found heterogeneous effects of mobile health interventions and called for larger trials with standardised outcomes [4]. Statistical guidance for clinical prediction and trial reporting highlights the need for pre-specified analyses [6].

## 3. Methods

We conducted a two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial in eight primary care clinics. Adults aged 30 to 75 years with a diagnosis of hypertension and a clinic systolic blood pressure above 140 mmHg were eligible. Participants were recruited during routine visits between January 2023 and March 2024. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated blocks stratified by clinic. The intervention group received a smartphone application providing weekly coaching messages, medication reminders and feedback on home blood pressure readings. The control group received usual care.

Blood pressure was measured with validated automated devices by staff blinded to allocation, using the mean of three repeated measurements at each visit. The primary outcome was the change in systolic blood pressure from baseline to six months. Secondary outcomes were medication adherence and the frequency of home monitoring. The sample size of 400 provided 90% power to detect a difference of 4 mmHg. Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle using linear mixed models adjusted for clinic, and we report 95% confidence intervals.

## 4. Results

Of 412 randomized patients, 389 completed the six-month visit (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics were balanced between groups. Systolic blood pressure decreased by 9.4 mmHg in the intervention group and by 4.1 mmHg in the control group. The adjusted difference was 5.3 mmHg (95% confidence interval 3.1 to 7.5, p < 0.001). Diastolic blood pressure decreased by 2.2 mmHg more in the intervention group. Medication adherence scores improved by 0.8 points compared with control, and home monitoring frequency doubled. No serious adverse events related to the intervention were reported.

## 5. Discussion

The coaching programme produced a clinically meaningful reduction in systolic blood pressure over six months. The effect size is similar to adding a second antihypertensive drug at standard dose. Improvements in adherence and self-monitoring are plausible mechanisms. A limitation of this trial is the six-month follow-up, which does not establish whether effects persist. Participants needed a smartphone, which may limit generalisability to older or low-income populations.

## 6. Conclusion

A smartphone coaching programme lowered systolic blood pressure by 5.3 mmHg more than usual care over six months and could be integrated into primary care.
